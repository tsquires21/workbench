No. 01 · The Garden

A yard that knows where the sun lands.

Every seed packet says "full sun." But how many hours does the back corner really get in June? What about in March, when the maple has no leaves yet? Garden Helper is the app I built to run our garden: every bed and plant on one screen, a harvest log that fills itself in, and a sun map that answers those questions for every square yard of the yard.

What it does
one

The whole garden on one screen

Every bed is a wooden box, and every plant is a tile. Tap a bed's name to open it up. You'll see how much sun it gets, and you can drag the plants around until the layout matches the real bed.

Built to be used with dirty hands: big targets, one tap, no forms.

two

Tap to harvest. Hold to learn.

Tap a tomato to log that you picked it. Press and hold any plant for its card: the variety, how many you've harvested so far, and notes on when it's ready. The harvest log fills itself in as you go, so by the end of the season there's a real record of what each bed produced. Or snap a photo of the basket: it waits in a short review list with a first guess at what and how much, drawn from past pickings, and nothing counts until I say OK.

The home screen also shows today's weather and a watering reminder for the week, and there are about 40 variety profiles behind the tiles.

three

Drag a bed. See its sunlight.

The sun map puts the garden on top of a real aerial photo of the yard. Drag a bed to where it actually sits, and it tells you how many hours of direct sun that spot gets. Flip the switch and the whole yard becomes a heat map, running from dark shade to gold sun all day.

The yard was never measured with a tape. The outlines of the house and shop come from public data, and they land on the roofs in the photo within about 15 centimetres.

four

Watch the shadows move

This is what the model sees, hour by hour, across the neighbourhood on 21 June. Navy is full shade, brown is sun filtered through leaves, and cream is direct sun. Press play and watch the shadows swing from west to east as the sun crosses the sky.

Add up every minute of the day and you get the sun-hours map. Do the same for January and the answer is completely different.

Same yard, two seasons. Scroll down for January.

five

Where the heights come from

To know what casts a shadow, you need to know how tall everything is. In 2018 the US Geological Survey flew a plane over the region, firing laser pulses at the ground and timing each bounce. That's called LiDAR, and the data is free to anyone.

The app downloads the points for this block and turns them into a height map of every roof, fence and treetop. The picture on the left is that height map, shaded as if the sun were low in the northwest.

six

Bugs, blights, and the folklore problem

Snap a photo of a spotty leaf and an AI model suggests what's going on. Behind that is a field guide I researched for Kansas City vegetables, covering early blight, squash vine borers, powdery mildew and more, with organic fixes for each.

The pest research turned up something useful: a lot of companion-planting advice is folklore. "Plant basil to repel hornworms" doesn't hold up in field trials. What does hold up is planting flowers that feed the good bugs, and using trap crops. So the app labels each tip as evidence-based, promising, or myth.

The calculators handle the rest: soil mix, rain barrel sizing, frost dates, seed-starting dates and a whole fall planting plan, all tuned to zone 6b.

seven

June versus January

The same neighborhood, as total hours of sun for the day. Bright yellow is a full day of light, and dark brown is almost none. In winter the low sun lets every tree and house throw a long shadow, so the sunny patches shrink to islands.

This is why a bed that's perfect for tomatoes can be useless for winter greens.

Ray tracingShade, per cellA ray from each square toward the sun: blocked, filtered, or clear
  • Surface and ground models. The point cloud becomes two rasters: everything the laser hit first (roofs, canopy) and the bare ground underneath. The difference is the height of what's in the way.
  • Trees aren't walls. A roof blocks the sun completely. A tree crown lets some through, and how much changes with the season, using leaf-on and leaf-off dates for zone 6b species.
  • Confidence, not just a number. Each cell carries a low, medium or high confidence label, so a shaky estimate says so.
  • The photo underneath is USDA NAIP aerial imagery: public domain, about 30 cm per pixel, and lined up three independent ways.
  • Python with NumPy and rasterio for the geometry, a small Flask server, and plain HTML on the phone. It runs on a laptop at home.

The laser scan is from 2018, and the yard isn't. When I checked the map against the real yard, it still showed a tree along the east fence that was cut down years ago, and a solid lump that turned out to be an old swingset. The covered deck had been read as "trees" and was being modelled as a leafy roof that lets 85% of the light through.

The model is only as current as the last flight over the house, and nothing free fixes that. So I built a way to correct it. The felled tree and the swingset are now marked as gone, and the deck is modelled as the solid roof it is. Every shadow on this page includes those fixes, and the map still says how confident it is, cell by cell.

Where it's headed
garden helper · final vision (design mockup)

The next version puts today's tasks, the weather, and smart alerts right beside the bed map. Past that, it grows into a whole-yard plan: beds, berries, a root cellar, rain capture, and a season calendar that tells you what to do this week.

I design with clickable mockups like these before anything gets built, so the decisions get made while changing them is still cheap.

Could this work for you?

Swap the tomatoes for solar panels, a patio, or a new tree, and it's the same question: how much sun does this exact spot really get, month by month? The same free public data can answer it for most addresses.
